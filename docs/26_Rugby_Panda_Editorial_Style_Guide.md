# The Rugby Panda Editorial Style Guide

## Status

This document is the canonical editorial source of truth for article generation, editorial review, scoring and future copy-editing tools.

## Mission

The Rugby Panda explains Irish and European rugby with accuracy, judgement and personality. Every article should help the reader understand not only what happened, but why it matters.

## Editorial philosophy

- Facts first; judgement follows evidence.
- Explain consequence, context and tactical meaning.
- Write for informed rugby supporters without excluding newer readers.
- Prefer original synthesis over rewritten source narratives.
- Human editorial approval remains mandatory.

## Editorial values

Accuracy, independence, clarity, restraint, fairness, originality and accountability.

## Audience

Irish and European rugby supporters who value credible news, informed analysis and an experienced but conversational voice.

## Tone and personality

- Informed, direct and confident without pretending certainty.
- Conversational rather than corporate.
- Wry humour is welcome when natural, never forced or cruel.
- Enthusiasm must not become hype.
- Criticism should be specific, fair and grounded.

## Writing rhythm

- Vary sentence length and openings naturally.
- Prefer clear active constructions.
- Keep paragraphs focused and usually short.
- Avoid mechanically uniform paragraph lengths or repeated sentence patterns.
- Use em dashes sparingly.

## Openings

Lead with the most important consequence, development or tension. Do not begin with generic scene-setting, dictionary definitions, broad history or empty declarations that a match was important.

## Headlines

- Be specific, accurate and compelling.
- Reflect the article's real angle.
- Avoid clickbait, unsupported certainty and generic hype.
- Prefer consequence and meaning over spectacle.

## Standfirsts

Add useful context rather than repeating the headline. State what changed, why it matters and what the reader will learn.

## Article structure

1. Lead with the central development or consequence.
2. Establish the verified facts and relevant context.
3. Explain why those facts matter.
4. Add grounded tactical, selection or competition analysis where supported.
5. Close with the next meaningful question or consequence, not a generic summary.

## News writing

Prioritise verified developments, timing, affected people or teams and immediate consequences. Separate confirmed facts from interpretation.

## Match reports

Report the decisive passages and explain how the contest changed. Do not produce a flat chronological event list. Scores, timings and quotations must be verified.

## Tactical analysis

Use rugby terminology precisely. Connect claims to observable evidence such as territory, possession, kicking strategy, set piece, breakdown, defensive shape, substitutions or discipline. Do not invent motives or tactical instructions.

## Opinion

Make the argument explicit, support it with facts, acknowledge material counterarguments and distinguish judgement from reporting.

## Humour

Use light, informed humour sparingly. Never use humour to obscure facts, belittle individuals or manufacture a house style.

## Irish English

Use Irish/British spelling and punctuation conventions, including forms such as `organise`, `defence`, `licence` as a noun and `programme` where appropriate. Avoid unnecessary Americanisms.

## Rugby terminology

Use accepted rugby-union language accurately: scrum, lineout, breakdown, maul, ruck, gain line, touch, half-back, first receiver, openside, blindside, tighthead, loosehead, back row and set piece. Avoid football or generic-sports terminology where rugby language is clearer.

## SEO

- Serve the reader first.
- Include important teams, competitions, players or events naturally.
- Ensure the SEO title and description accurately reflect the article.
- Avoid keyword stuffing, duplicated phrasing and misleading search hooks.

## Originality

Articles must be original synthesis based on the approved fact ledger and sources. Do not imitate a source's structure, distinctive phrasing or sequence of argument. Quotes must remain attributed and materially accurate.

## Prohibited clichés and hype

Avoid phrases such as:

- clash of titans
- massive statement
- epic encounter
- mouth-watering clash
- sent shockwaves
- wrote their name into history
- left everything on the pitch
- game of two halves

Equivalent generic hype should also be challenged.

## Common AI fingerprints

Avoid:

- `Overall`
- `In conclusion`
- `It is worth noting`
- `Furthermore`
- `Moreover`
- `Not only ... but also ...`
- repeated sentence openings
- repetitive paragraph length
- excessive signposting
- vague claims without actors, evidence or consequence
- generic praise or criticism
- summaries that merely restate preceding paragraphs

## Accuracy requirements

- Never invent facts, quotations, statistics, injuries, selections, motives or certainty.
- Use only facts supported by the retained fact ledger and sources.
- Flag conflicts, missing support and required human checks.
- Distinguish verified accuracy from internal consistency.
- A high review score is not proof that every fact has been independently verified.

## Quote handling

Preserve the speaker, meaning and material wording. Do not manufacture, merge or polish quotations into words the speaker did not use. Attribute every quotation and retain its source.

## Speculation rules

Label inference and uncertainty clearly. Use formulations such as `may`, `could`, `appears` or `suggests` only when justified. Do not present prediction, motive or tactical inference as confirmed fact.
